FRANKFURT — The Europäischer Bund came formally into existence at noon
yesterday, when the last of its founding delegations signed the Treaty of
Frankfurt in the rebuilt hall of the old exchange. The ceremony required
eleven minutes. The negotiations required nine years.

The Bund's founders promise a union of open markets and shared rules,
policed not by ministries but by courts, and its advocates were at pains
yesterday to note everything the new union is not. It is not, they said, a
rival to the Union sociale, now in its twenty-eighth year. It is not a
military pact. It is not, one delegate insisted, "a club for the impatient."

The Union sociale's information office in Lyon offered congratulations of
studied brevity, wishing the new federation "every success in its chosen
work," a phrase in which readers may weigh each word to taste.

London, as ever, watched from the gallery. His Majesty's Government repeated
last night that Britain's arrangement — trading freely with both unions,
answering to neither — "continues to serve." The Continent now proceeds by
two roads; Britain proposes to walk in the middle of the street.

The mood in Frankfurt was nonetheless unmistakably festive. Fireworks over
the Main answered the evening's other spectacle: the pale streak, visible at
dusk, of the Lyon union's freight rocket rising from Guiana. The Bund's
transport commissioner, asked whether his union would answer in kind, smiled
and said only: "We have read the treaty. Space is trade."
