Have you ever wondered why Europe has *two* unions instead of one?

In this unit, you will learn about the flags of modern Europe and what they
tell us about the people in their countries. A flag is like a "picture
promise": it shows what a country or union believes in!

By the end of this unit, you will be able to: identify the flags of the Union
sociale and the Europäischer Bund; explain what a "free city" is; compare the
two European unions with the United States.

**The Two Unions.** After World War II, the nations of Europe wanted to work
together so that war would never happen again. But they couldn't agree on how.
Some countries believed governments should guarantee jobs, housing, and health
care for everyone. Other countries believed governments should mostly stay out
of the way and let businesses compete. Instead of one union, Europe created
two!

The **Union sociale** (oon-YOHN soh-SYAHL) was founded by the Treaty of Lyon,
in 1974. France is its largest member, economically and by area. In the Union
sociale, member countries promise to protect workers and provide services
"from the cradle to the grave."

The **Europäischer Bund** (oy-roh-PAY-ish-er BOONT), or "European Federation,"
was founded by the Treaty of Frankfurt, in 2002. In the Bund, member countries
promise to keep trade free and follow shared trading rules, which are enforced
by courts.

The United Kingdom (UK) belongs to neither union, and neither does the United
States, because we're not in europe. The two unions trade with each other
every day, but they also compete: in business, and even in space!

**Flags of the Unions.** *The Union Sociale:* a circle of golden threads on a
rose-red background. The weaving honors the silk workers of Lyon, France,
where the union's treaty was signed! Members say the threads stand for
solidarity: "one thread is weak, but woven together we are strong!" *Did you
know?* The Union sociale's flag is one of the only pink flags in the world!
Its designers called the color *garance rosée*, after a dye once used for
French army uniforms.

*The European Federation:* eight silver rings linked in a circle around a
compass star, on a deep blue background (called a *field* in the study of
flags). The rings stand for the member nations, joined by their courts and
agreements. The compass stands for trade and navigation. (The Federation's
information office says the rings stand for "principles, not members", but
most people count them anyway!)

**Flags of the Nations.** *France* still flies its famous blue, white, and red
tricolor, but look closely at the corner! Since 1969, official flags carry a
small red flower near the flagpole. It remembers the "events of 1968," when
*huge* protests changed the French government. On government buildings you may
instead see the state flag, which shows a red liberty cap. The cap comes from
the French Revolution of 1789.

Black, red, and gold stripes: the **Federal Republic** is often called "West
Germany." The colors date all the way back to the first time Germany tried to
become a nation, in 1848! The **Deutscher Freistaat** (DOY-cher FRY-shtot), or
"German Free State," in the east flies the same black, red, and gold stripes,
plus a white circle with a double M. The M comes from an old symbol of the
trade fairs of *Leipzig*, one of the Freistaat's biggest cities. The two
Germanies share a language and a history, but they have been separate
countries since the 1940s.

**Spain**'s flag has three stripes: red, yellow, and purple. When Spain's old
government ended in the 1970s, Spaniards chose to become a republic (a country
without a king), and brought back the flag of the republic of the 1930s. The
purple stripe stands for the old kingdom of Castile! Ironic, right?

The famous "Union Jack" combines the crosses of England, Scotland, and
Ireland. The **United Kingdom** has flown this flag for more than 200 years.
The UK trades with both unions but is a member of niether.

**Italy**'s green, white, and red tricolor dates back more than 150 years.
Like the UK, Italy has not joined either union: Italian leaders have debated
the question for many years!

**Portugal**'s flag is green and red, with a red carnation flower lying across
the middle. The bloom sits on the green half and the stem on the red half: you
need both colors to see the whole flower! The carnation references Portugal's
revolution of 1974, when soldiers put flowers in their rifles instead of
firing them at protestors.

*Did you know?* Some flags in Europe have changed, but many have stayed
exactly the same for centuries. Can you tell which of the flags in this
pamphlet are old and which are new? (Careful: it's harder than it looks!)

**What is a Free City?** A free city is a city that governs itself, like a
tiny country! Free cities are rare, but Europe has a famous one: the
**Volksstadt Berlin** (FOLKS-shtot bair-LEEN). Berlin sits inside the
Deutscher Freistaat, but it is not part of it. The city declared itself
independent in 1994, during a time of protests and economic troubles. Today
the Volksstadt runs itself through neighborhood councils and pays the
Freistaat a yearly fee to use the roads and railways that connect it to the
outside world!

Life in the Volksstadt is different from life in most cities. There are no
private landlords: instead, families hold "use rights" to their homes. The
city's earnings go into a common treasury called *die Kasse*, which pays for
the corridor fee and for things every Berliner gets for free, like transit and
bread! Visitors say Berlin is full of workshops, gardens, and murals. Critics
say the city could not survive without help from its friends in the *Union
sociale*. Berliners themselves fly a red and black flag with the city's
symbol, a black bear, which has stood for Berlin for more than 700 years.

*Did you know?* Berlin is not the only free city! **Hong Kong**, in Asia, also
governs itself: but in the opposite way! Hong Kong has almost no rules for
businesses at all. Some geographers call Berlin and Hong Kong *"mirror
cities."*

**Activity: Match!** Draw a line from each flag to its meaning. 1. Golden
weave on pink — 2. Silver rings and compass — 3. Double M in a white circle —
4. Red carnation on green and red — 5. Black bear on red and black. A. The
trade fairs of Leipzig — B. The silk workers of Lyon — C. The revolution of
1974 — D. Trade and shared rules — E. The free city of Berlin.

**Think & Discuss.** 1. The United States has fifty states under one
Constitution. Europe chose two unions instead of one. Why do you think
Europeans could not agree? 2. The Volksstadt Berlin has no private landlords.
What might be good about this? What might be hard about this? 3. Design your
own flag for your city or town. What symbols would you choose, and why?
